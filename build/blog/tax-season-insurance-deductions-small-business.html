Tax Season Insurance Deductions for Small Businesses

By The Way Agency, Independent Insurance Agency · January 27, 2027 · 6 min read

Tax season is not anyone's favorite time of year, but it does come with one bright spot for small business owners: most of the insurance premiums you pay to protect your business are tax-deductible.

If you are a Kentucky small business owner getting your records together for 2026 taxes, here is what you need to know about deducting your insurance costs, how to document them properly, and a few mistakes that are easy to avoid.

Which insurance premiums are deductible?

The IRS considers insurance premiums a deductible business expense when the coverage is ordinary and necessary for your trade or business. In practice, that covers almost every commercial policy you carry:

General liability insurance is deductible. This is the foundational policy that protects your business against third-party bodily injury, property damage, and advertising injury claims.

Commercial property insurance is deductible. Whether you own or lease your business space, the premiums you pay to protect your building, equipment, inventory, and business personal property are a legitimate business expense.

Workers compensation insurance is deductible. If you have employees in Kentucky, you are required to carry workers comp in most cases, and the premiums are fully deductible as a business expense.

Commercial auto insurance is deductible for vehicles used in your business. If a vehicle is used for both business and personal purposes, you can deduct the business-use portion of the premium.

Professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance is deductible. If you provide professional services and carry E&O coverage, those premiums are a business expense.

Cyber liability insurance is deductible. As more businesses face digital risks, this is becoming a standard policy, and the premiums are treated like any other business insurance expense.

Business interruption insurance is deductible. This coverage, which pays lost income when your business is shut down by a covered event, is considered ordinary and necessary.

Group health insurance premiums you pay on behalf of your employees are deductible. This is often one of the largest insurance expenses for small businesses that offer group coverage, and the full employer-paid portion is deductible.

What about self-employed health insurance?

If you are self-employed (sole proprietor, partner, LLC member, or S-corp shareholder owning more than 2 percent), you may be able to deduct health insurance premiums for yourself, your spouse, and your dependents. This is the self-employed health insurance deduction, and it works differently from other business insurance deductions:

  • It is taken as an adjustment to income on your personal tax return (Form 1040), not as a business expense on Schedule C.
  • It reduces your adjusted gross income, which can lower your overall tax burden.
  • You can only deduct up to your net self-employment income. If your business had a loss, you cannot use this deduction.
  • You cannot take this deduction if you were eligible for an employer-sponsored health plan (through a spouse's job, for example).

This is an area where your accountant's guidance is worth following closely, as the rules have specific conditions.

How to document your insurance deductions

Good documentation is straightforward. You need:

  • Premium statements or invoices from your insurance carrier or agent showing the amount paid and the coverage period.
  • Proof of payment. Bank statements or canceled checks showing the premiums were actually paid.
  • Policy declarations pages showing the type of coverage and the named insured (your business).

    • Keep these records for at least three years from the date you file your return, though seven years is a safer practice. Digital copies are fine. Most carriers and agents can provide duplicate documents if you need them.

    Common mistakes to avoid

    Mixing business and personal policies

    If you use your personal vehicle for business, you cannot deduct the entire auto insurance premium as a business expense. You can only deduct the portion attributable to business use. The same applies to a home office: if you run your business from home, the business-use percentage of your homeowners or renters insurance may be deductible, but not the full amount.

    Keep clean records of business use versus personal use. The IRS expects a reasonable allocation method, not guesswork.

    Forgetting to deduct workers comp

    Some small business owners overlook workers compensation premiums, especially if they use a payroll service that bundles the cost. Workers comp premiums are a separate deductible expense, and they can be significant, particularly in trades with higher risk classifications.

    Prepaying premiums and deducting them in the wrong year

    If you prepay a 12-month premium in December that covers January through December of the following year, you generally cannot deduct the full amount in the year you paid it. You deduct the portion that applies to each tax year. Cash-basis taxpayers have some flexibility here, but the 12-month rule has limits. Your accountant can advise on the specifics.

    Not deducting surety bond premiums

    If you are a contractor who pays for surety bonds, those premiums are deductible as a business expense. Many contractors forget about these or lump them in with other costs. They are a legitimate insurance-related deduction.

    Overlooking small policies

    Business insurance is not just the big-ticket policies. Inland marine coverage on your tools, a cyber liability policy, a hired and non-owned auto endorsement, these all have premiums that are deductible. Small policies add up over a tax year.

    How insurance deductions reduce your tax bill

    To put it in concrete terms: if your small business pays $12,000 per year in total insurance premiums and you are in the 22 percent federal tax bracket, deducting those premiums saves you $2,640 in federal income taxes. Add Kentucky's flat state income tax, and the savings increase further.

    That is not a reason to buy insurance you do not need. But it does mean the effective cost of your business insurance is lower than the sticker price, something worth remembering when you are evaluating coverage options.

    Organize now, save headaches later

    If you are scrambling to find insurance documents every January, set up a simple system:

    • Create a dedicated folder (physical or digital) for all business insurance documents.
    • When you receive a premium invoice, a declarations page, or a payment confirmation, put it in the folder immediately.
    • At year-end, your accountant has everything they need in one place.

    Talk to your accountant, not just your agent

    We can tell you what policies you have and what you paid in premiums. Your accountant or tax professional can tell you how to properly classify and deduct those expenses. Insurance agents are not tax advisors, and the tax code has nuances that matter.
