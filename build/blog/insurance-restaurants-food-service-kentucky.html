Insurance for Restaurants and Food Service Businesses in Kentucky

By The Way Agency, Independent Insurance Agency · November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Running a restaurant in Kentucky is rewarding and demanding. Between managing staff, sourcing ingredients, and keeping customers happy, insurance probably is not at the top of your daily to-do list. But restaurants face a unique combination of risks that most other businesses do not — and a single claim can shut your doors permanently if you are not properly covered.

Grease fires. Slip-and-fall injuries. Foodborne illness claims. Liquor liability. Employee injuries from burns and cuts. Equipment breakdowns that spoil thousands of dollars in inventory. These are not hypothetical risks — they happen to Kentucky restaurants regularly.

Here is what you need to know about protecting your food service business.

The insurance every Kentucky restaurant needs

General liability insurance is the foundation. It covers third-party bodily injury and property damage claims. When a customer slips on a wet floor, trips on a loose threshold, or gets hit by a swinging kitchen door, general liability pays for their medical bills and any resulting lawsuit. Most landlords and commercial leases require at least $1 million in general liability coverage.

Commercial property insurance protects your physical assets — kitchen equipment, dining furniture, signage, inventory, and the building itself if you own it. Restaurants have a high concentration of expensive, specialized equipment, and replacing a commercial oven, walk-in cooler, or hood system is not cheap.

Business income coverage replaces your lost revenue if a covered event forces you to close. If a fire shuts down your kitchen for two months, business income coverage keeps paying your fixed costs — rent, loan payments, and employee wages — while you rebuild. Without it, many restaurant owners would not survive the closure.

Workers' compensation insurance is required in Kentucky for businesses with one or more employees. Restaurants have high rates of workplace injuries: burns from grills and fryers, cuts from knives and slicers, slips on wet kitchen floors, and repetitive strain injuries. Workers' comp pays for medical treatment and lost wages when an employee is hurt on the job.

Liquor liability insurance is essential if you serve alcohol. Kentucky's dram shop laws can hold your restaurant liable if you serve alcohol to a visibly intoxicated person who then injures someone or causes property damage. Standard general liability policies typically exclude liquor-related claims, so you need a separate liquor liability policy or endorsement.

Coverage that restaurants often overlook

Food contamination and spoilage coverage. If your walk-in cooler fails overnight and you lose $5,000 in perishable inventory, standard property insurance may not cover it. Food spoilage coverage pays for inventory lost due to equipment failure or power outages. In Kentucky, where summer storms can knock out power for hours or days, this coverage matters.

Equipment breakdown coverage. Commercial kitchen equipment takes a beating. When your HVAC system, refrigeration unit, or commercial dishwasher breaks down mechanically (not from an external event like a fire), equipment breakdown coverage pays for repairs or replacement. Standard property policies typically exclude mechanical breakdown.

Hired and non-owned auto coverage. If your employees use their personal vehicles for deliveries, catering runs, or supply pickups, your business is liable for accidents that occur during those trips. Hired and non-owned auto coverage protects your business without requiring a full commercial auto policy.

Cyber liability. If your restaurant processes credit card payments — and nearly all of them do — you are a target for data breaches. A breach that exposes customer payment data can result in notification costs, forensic investigation fees, and regulatory fines. Cyber liability coverage handles these costs.

Umbrella insurance. A serious injury claim — a customer's allergic reaction, a delivery accident, a major slip-and-fall — can exceed your general liability limits quickly. An umbrella policy provides an extra $1 million or more above your underlying coverage.

Kentucky-specific restaurant insurance considerations

Health department compliance. Kentucky restaurants are inspected by local health departments. While insurance does not replace compliance, maintaining proper coverage and safety procedures can reduce your risk profile and help with claims if something goes wrong.

Seasonal operations. If your restaurant is seasonal or your revenue fluctuates significantly throughout the year, make sure your business income coverage reflects your actual earnings pattern. A policy based on annual averages may underestimate your coverage needs during peak months.

Catering and off-premises events. If your restaurant caters events at other locations, your general liability policy needs to cover off-premises operations. Some policies restrict coverage to your primary business location, so check the terms if catering is part of your revenue.

Food trucks. If you operate a food truck in addition to a brick-and-mortar restaurant, you need commercial auto insurance for the vehicle, plus a general liability extension that covers your mobile operations. Food trucks have their own unique insurance requirements.

How much does restaurant insurance cost in Kentucky?

Restaurant insurance costs depend on your type of operation, location, number of employees, annual revenue, and whether you serve alcohol. Here are rough ranges:

  • General liability: $800 to $3,000 per year
  • Commercial property: $1,000 to $5,000 per year (depending on equipment value and building)
  • Workers' compensation: Varies by payroll and classification. Restaurant employees are typically classified at higher rates due to injury frequency.
  • Liquor liability: $300 to $2,000 per year depending on alcohol sales volume
  • Business Owner's Policy (BOP): $1,500 to $5,000 per year (bundles property and liability)

A full-service restaurant with a bar, 20 employees, and $1 million in annual revenue might pay $8,000 to $15,000 per year in total insurance costs. A small cafe with five employees and no alcohol service might pay $3,000 to $6,000.

Reducing your restaurant insurance costs

You can not eliminate risk in a restaurant, but you can manage it:

  • Invest in safety training. Documented safety programs for kitchen staff reduce workplace injuries and can lower your workers' comp premiums.
  • Maintain your equipment. Regular maintenance reduces the likelihood of equipment failures and fires.
  • Document everything. Incident reports, maintenance logs, and training records strengthen your position if a claim occurs.
  • Install fire suppression systems. Commercial kitchen fire suppression systems (above grills and fryers) are often required by code and can reduce your property insurance premiums.
  • Review your coverage annually. Restaurants change — new menus, new locations, new employees. Make sure your insurance keeps up.

Getting the right coverage for your restaurant

Restaurant insurance is not a single policy. It is a combination of coverages tailored to your specific operation. A fine-dining establishment with a full bar has different needs than a quick-service sandwich shop. A multi-location franchise has different needs than a single family-owned restaurant.
