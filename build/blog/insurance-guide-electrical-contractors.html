Insurance Guide for Electrical Contractors in Kentucky

By The Way Agency, Independent Insurance Agency · February 24, 2027 · 7 min read

Electrical work is one of the highest-risk trades in construction. Faulty wiring can cause fires years after the work is done. Arc flashes can injure workers on the job. A miswired panel in a commercial building can create liability that follows you for decades.

Insurance carriers know this, which is why electrical contractor insurance costs more than many other trades and why getting the right coverage in place is critical. If you are running an electrical contracting business in Kentucky, here is what you need, what it costs, and how to set it up so you can focus on the work.

Required coverage for Kentucky electrical contractors

General liability insurance

General liability is the foundation of your coverage as an electrical contractor. It protects your business against third-party claims for bodily injury, property damage, and completed operations.

For electricians, the "completed operations" piece is especially important. If wiring you installed causes a fire six months after the job is done, your completed operations coverage responds. Without it, you are personally on the hook for the damage, the injuries, and the legal costs.

Most general contractors require electrical subs to carry at least $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate in general liability. Many commercial projects and larger GCs require $2 million per occurrence. Your policy limits need to match the contracts you are bidding on.

Typical cost for an electrical contractor: $2,500 to $6,000 per year for a small operation, depending on your revenue, payroll, and claims history.

Workers compensation insurance

Kentucky requires workers compensation insurance for most employers with one or more employees. Electrical work carries a higher workers comp classification rate due to the inherent risk of electrocution, falls, burns, and musculoskeletal injuries.

Workers comp covers medical expenses, lost wages, and rehabilitation for employees injured on the job. It also protects you from lawsuits by injured employees, since workers comp is the exclusive remedy in most cases.

Your workers comp premium is based on your payroll and your experience modification rate (mod rate). A clean safety record brings your mod rate below 1.0 and reduces your premium. Claims drive it up.

Typical cost for electrical contractors: $8 to $15 per $100 of payroll, depending on your classification code, mod rate, and state. Kentucky's workers comp rates for electricians are moderate compared to some states, but the cost is still significant for labor-intensive operations.

Commercial auto insurance

If your business owns vehicles, commercial auto insurance is required. This covers your trucks, vans, and any vehicles titled to the business. If your employees drive personal vehicles for work (picking up materials, traveling to job sites), you also need hired and non-owned auto coverage to fill the gap when personal auto policies exclude business use.

Typical cost: $1,500 to $4,000 per vehicle per year, depending on the vehicle type, driver records, and coverage limits.

Coverage you should carry

Inland marine (tools and equipment)

Your tools are your livelihood. Wire pullers, benders, meters, testing equipment, and power tools are expensive to replace, and they spend most of their time in your truck or on a job site, not in a secure shop.

Inland marine insurance (sometimes called a contractors equipment floater) covers your tools and equipment for theft, damage, and loss wherever they are. A standard commercial property policy only covers items at your business location. Inland marine follows your equipment to the job.

Typical cost: $300 to $1,000 per year, depending on the total value of covered equipment.

Umbrella or excess liability

An umbrella policy adds an additional layer of liability coverage above your general liability, auto, and workers comp limits. For electrical contractors, this is worth serious consideration.

Electrical work has long-tail liability. A wiring defect can cause a fire years after installation. If the resulting damage exceeds your general liability limits, an umbrella policy picks up the excess. A $1 million umbrella policy for an electrical contractor typically costs $500 to $1,500 per year.

Professional liability (errors and omissions)

If you do design work, provide engineering services, or advise clients on system specifications, professional liability insurance covers claims arising from errors in your professional judgment. This is separate from general liability, which covers physical damage and injury.

Not every electrician needs professional liability, but if your contracts include design-build work or you sign off on system designs, it is worth discussing with your agent.

Meeting general contractor requirements

Most of your work probably comes through general contractors, and every GC has insurance requirements for their subs. Here is what you will typically need to provide:

  • Certificate of insurance showing your general liability, workers comp, and auto coverage with limits that meet the GC's requirements
  • Additional insured endorsement naming the GC (and sometimes the project owner) as an additional insured on your general liability policy
  • Waiver of subrogation on your workers comp and general liability policies in favor of the GC
  • Primary and non-contributory language stating that your coverage responds first, before the GC's coverage

These are standard requirements. Your agent should be able to process certificate requests and endorsements quickly, often the same day. If you are regularly waiting days for certificates, that is a sign your agency is not set up for contractor accounts.

If you are unfamiliar with certificates and endorsements, our posts on certificates of insurance and how they work cover the basics.

How electrical contractor insurance is priced

Carriers look at several factors when pricing your coverage:

Revenue and payroll. Higher revenue and payroll mean higher premiums because they represent more exposure. Your general liability premium is typically based on revenue, while workers comp is based on payroll.

Type of work. Residential wiring, commercial electrical, industrial installations, and low-voltage work (data cabling, fire alarm, security) all have different risk profiles. Industrial and high-voltage work costs more to insure than residential.

Claims history. Your loss history over the past three to five years directly affects your pricing. Clean history gets better rates. Frequent claims, especially serious ones, can make it difficult to find coverage at all.

Experience modification rate. Your workers comp mod rate reflects your claims history relative to similar businesses. A mod rate below 1.0 saves you money. A mod rate above 1.0 means you are paying more than the baseline.

Safety programs. Carriers reward businesses that take safety seriously. A written safety program, regular toolbox talks, OSHA training documentation, and drug testing can all contribute to lower premiums.

Reducing your insurance costs

There is no trick to cheap electrical contractor insurance, but there are legitimate ways to manage costs:

Invest in safety. Every claim you prevent saves you money directly through lower premiums and indirectly through lower deductibles, less downtime, and a better mod rate. OSHA 10 and OSHA 30 training for your team is a good starting point.

Classify your work correctly. Different types of electrical work have different classification codes and rates. Make sure your policies reflect what you actually do. If you are classified as a high-voltage industrial electrician but most of your work is residential, you may be overpaying.

Shop with an independent agent. Not every carrier wants to write electrical contractor insurance. An independent agent knows which carriers are competitive for your class of business and can shop your coverage across multiple options. We work with contractors in similar trades, including HVAC contractors, and understand how to present your business to carriers.

Bundle your policies. Carrying your general liability, workers comp, auto, and inland marine with one carrier or through one agency often yields better pricing and simpler administration.

Maintain accurate records. Accurate payroll and revenue reporting prevents surprises at audit time. If your actual payroll is significantly higher than what you estimated, you will owe additional premium at audit. Report accurately from the start.

Frequently asked questions

Total insurance costs for a small electrical contracting business in Kentucky typically range from $8,000 to $20,000 per year, depending on payroll, revenue, number of employees, type of work, and claims history. Workers compensation is usually the largest expense, followed by general liability.

Kentucky allows sole proprietors with no employees to exempt themselves from workers comp requirements. However, many general contractors require all subcontractors to carry workers comp regardless of exemption status. Without workers comp, a job-site injury comes out of your own pocket.

Most GCs require general liability ($1M/$2M or higher), workers compensation, commercial auto, and often an umbrella policy. They will also require a certificate of insurance naming them as additional insured with a waiver of subrogation. Specific limits vary by GC and project.
